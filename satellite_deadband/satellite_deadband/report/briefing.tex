\title{\textbf{Manoeuvre Pattern-of-Life Prediction for Space Domain Awareness}\\[4pt]
\large Learning a station-keeping control law from noisy tracking data:\\ drag estimation, Gaussian-mixture
burn detection, SINDy/BINDy and Gaussian-process models, and an end-to-end assessment}
\author{UROP project, Imperial College London}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
\noindent A Starlink-like satellite keeps its mean semi-major axis (SMA) inside a 500\,m deadband by firing a
low-thrust engine whenever the SMA decays to the lower edge. We simulate 400 days of this behaviour with
two-body gravity, $J_2$, drag, lunar and solar attraction and solar radiation pressure, and then try to recover
everything an observer would need to \emph{predict} the next manoeuvre from noisy state measurements alone.
The analysis is a four-stage chain: (1) estimate the drag coefficient $c_d$ (finite-difference regression,
energy method, shooting, extended Kalman filter, spectral ``force spectroscopy''); (2) detect the burns with a
Gaussian mixture model (GMM) on the unmodelled acceleration; (3) learn the natural and thrust dynamics and the
switching law with sparse identification (SINDy), its Bayesian variant (BINDy) and Gaussian processes (GP);
(4) forecast future burns. Each stage is scored against the truth, an error budget swaps the truth into one
stage at a time, every stage is re-run at sampling steps from 6\,s to 10\,min, and 16 headline numbers are
checked against closed-form formulae (all agree within 1\%, or obey their bound). The GMM finds all 92 test
burns with no false events from 6-s to 10-min data; the drag coefficient is recovered to 0.03--0.08\% at every
sampling step; learning the thrust law and the natural law \emph{separately} cuts the year-ahead forecast error
from 23 hours (joint regression) to about one hour; the band edges, not the physics coefficients, dominate the
remaining error. With variable space weather a learned law with a drag proxy halves the error of a periodic
schedule, and the forecasts reduce missed conjunctions, lost tracks and sensor time. The GMM's skewed coast cloud is
explained from first principles (the log of a 3-D noise vector has a fat exponential left tail), and a physics-shaped
mixture with 10 parameters beats the 23-parameter GMM on BIC. With the Moon, Sun and SRP in the truth, detection is
unchanged but the year-ahead forecast error doubles, and modelling them recovers most of it. Methods are ranked for
each stage of the pipeline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================================================
\subsection{The brief}
The UK Space Agency challenge ``Manoeuvre Pattern of Life Prediction for SDA'' \cite{uksa} starts from the
growth of the active satellite population: of $\sim$50\,000 tracked objects about 15\,000 are active and may
manoeuvre, and mega-constellations will multiply this by 2035. Unpredicted manoeuvres break three core Space
Domain Awareness (SDA) functions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{close-approach (conjunction) screening}, which assumes predicted trajectories are accurate over
        the screening window --- in the brief's Figure~1 a Starlink satellite keeping its SMA inside a 500\,m
        deadband moves its miss distance by \emph{hundreds of km in a week} \cite{vallado2025};
  \item \textbf{track association}, because a manoeuvre makes the predicted position wrong, so new observations
        cannot be linked to the right catalogue object \cite{barshalom};
  \item \textbf{sensor scheduling}, because the uncertainty of an unpredicted manoeuvre has to be removed by
        tasking extra observations \cite{marsillach}.
\end{enumerate}
Detecting \emph{past} manoeuvres is well studied; the gap the brief identifies is predicting \emph{future}
manoeuvres by \textbf{reconstructing the control law from time series of measurements} \cite{leuridan}.

\subsection{The question and the approach}
\begin{keybox}{Question}
From noisy position and velocity data, can the station-keeping law of a satellite (``burn when the mean SMA
reaches the lower edge, stop at the upper edge'') be learned well enough to forecast its future burns --- and
which method is best at each step of that chain?
\end{keybox}
The approach is a controlled experiment: a simulator whose truth is known, a chain of estimators each of which
can be scored against that truth, and studies of how the chain degrades with noise, data length and sampling.
Figure~\ref{fig:chain} shows the chain; Sections~\ref{sec:cd}--\ref{sec:forecast} follow it stage by stage.

\section{Simulation: physics, controller and data}\label{sec:sim}
% =====================================================================================================
\subsection{Equations of motion}
The state is $\mathbf x = (\mathbf r,\mathbf v)$, plus a thruster state $s$ for the controlled runs:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf v,\qquad
  \dot{\mathbf v} = -\frac{\mu\,\mathbf r}{r^3} + \mathbf a_{J_2} + \mathbf a_{\text{drag}} + \mathbf a_{\text{Moon}}
   + \mathbf a_{\text{Sun}} + \mathbf a_{\text{SRP}} + \mathbf a_{\text{thrust}} .
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Oblateness.} With the \emph{equatorial} radius $R_e = 6378.137$\,km and $J_2 = 1.08263\times10^{-3}$
\cite{vallado_book},
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf a_{J_2} = \frac{3}{2}\frac{J_2\,\mu R_e^2}{r^4}
  \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{x}{r}(5\tfrac{z^2}{r^2}-1)\\ \tfrac{y}{r}(5\tfrac{z^2}{r^2}-1)\\ \tfrac{z}{r}(5\tfrac{z^2}{r^2}-3)\end{pmatrix}
  = -\nabla U_{J_2},\qquad U_{J_2} = \frac{\mu J_2 R_e^2}{2 r^3}(3\sin^2\phi - 1).
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Drag.} A lumped ballistic coefficient, $\mathbf a_{\text{drag}} = -c_d|\mathbf v|\mathbf v$, with constant
density in steps 1--9 (time-varying in steps 10--16). Its value gives 5.3\,m/hour of SMA decay at 550\,km.
\paragraph{Third bodies and SRP.} Tidal attraction $\mu_b\big((\mathbf r_b-\mathbf r)/|\mathbf r_b-\mathbf r|^3 -
\mathbf r_b/r_b^3\big)$ for the Moon and the Sun on circular orbits, and a Sun-facing flat plate for SRP with
$P C_R A/m = 4.56\,\mu\text{N/m}^2 \times 1.3 \times 5/260$\,m$^2$/kg \cite{montenbruck}.
\paragraph{Integration.} \code{DOP853} (8th-order Runge--Kutta) with $\text{rtol} = \text{atol} = 10^{-10}$
(non-dimensional) for the free runs; \code{LSODA} \cite{petzold} with $10^{-11}$ for the controlled runs, because the
tanh switches make the equations stiff for a few seconds at each burn edge. A cross-check over 2 days against a
$10^{-13}$ DOP853 reference gives maximum position differences of 0.27\,m (DOP853), 28\,m (LSODA) and 0.013\,m (Radau) at
$10^{-10}$, far below any effect studied.

\paragraph{The analytical comparison.} The simulated node is read from the angular momentum
$\mathbf h = \mathbf r\times\mathbf v$: $\Omega = \operatorname{atan2}(h_x, -h_y)$ (unwrapped) and $\cos i = h_z/|\mathbf h|$.
The theoretical node is the secular $J_2$ rate \cite{vallado_book} integrated along the run,
\begin{equation}
  \Omega_{\text{th}}(t) = \Omega(0) + \int_0^t \dot\Omega(\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad
  \dot\Omega(\tau) = -\frac{3}{2}\,\bar n(\tau)\,J_2\left(\frac{R_e}{\bar p(\tau)}\right)^2\cos\bar\imath(\tau),\qquad
  \bar n = \sqrt{\mu/\bar a^3},
\end{equation}
where $\bar a$ and $\bar\imath$ are running one-orbit means (95 one-minute samples) of the osculating SMA and
inclination, and $\bar p = \bar a(1-e^2)\approx\bar a$ because $e\approx10^{-4}$. The integral is a cumulative sum with
$\Delta t = 1$\,min. Evaluating the rate along the run, rather than once at $t=0$, matters: drag lowers the free orbit
by 51\,km, which speeds the regression up by 2.6\%.
\fig{step1_dynamics/panels/step1_j2_check_b.png}{Simulated minus theoretical node angle: 0.039\% of the total regression after 400 days.}{fig:j2diff}

\subsection{The station-keeping controller}
\paragraph{Why the mean SMA.} The osculating SMA $a_{\text{osc}} = -\mu/(2\varepsilon)$ with
$\varepsilon = v^2/2-\mu/r$ swings by 12\,km per orbit under $J_2$, 25 times the band. Adding the $J_2$ potential
to the energy removes the swing,
\begin{equation}
  a_{\text{mean}} = -\frac{\mu}{2\,\varepsilon_{J_2}},\qquad \varepsilon_{J_2} = \frac{v^2}{2} - \frac{\mu}{r} + U_{J_2}(\mathbf r),
\end{equation}
and $\varepsilon_{J_2}$ changes only through drag and thrust.
\fig{step5_deadband/panels/smooth_j2_mean_vs_osculating_a.png}{Osculating SMA (12\,km swing, twice per orbit) and the mean
SMA used by the controller (flat to centimetres between burns).}{fig:meanosc}
\fig{step5_deadband/panels/smooth_j2_mean_vs_osculating_c.png}{Simulated minus first-order theory for the osculating swing: 11\,m out of 12.2\,km.}{fig:osctheory}
\fig{step5_deadband/panels/smooth_j2_mean_vs_osculating_d.png}{A controller that triggers on the osculating SMA fires every orbit and climbs out of the band: why the mean SMA is needed.}{fig:osctrigger}

Short-period swing of the osculating SMA: peak to peak $3J_2R_e^2\sin^2 i/a = 12.17$\,km, against 12.22\,km in the
simulation (0.4\%; the formula is first order in $J_2$).
\end{checkbox}
\subsection{The thrust model: a piecewise law made smooth with sigmoids}\label{sec:thrustmodel}
\paragraph{The ideal (piecewise) law.} An operator's deadband law is a two-edge switch with memory:
\begin{equation}
  s(t^+) = \begin{cases} 1 & a_{\text{mean}} \le a_L \quad\text{(start a burn)}\\
                         0 & a_{\text{mean}} \ge a_U \quad\text{(stop the burn)}\\
                         s(t^-) & a_L < a_{\text{mean}} < a_U \quad\text{(keep doing what you were doing)}\end{cases},
  \qquad \mathbf a_{\text{thrust}} = T\,s\,\hat{\mathbf v}.
\end{equation}
Written with Heaviside steps $H$ this is $s^+ = H(a_L-a) + H(a-a_L)H(a_U-a)\,s^-$: discontinuous, so an ODE solver
can only handle it with event detection, and the thrust would jump from 0 to $T$ instantly.
\paragraph{The sigmoidal smoothing.} Every step $H(x)$ is replaced by the sigmoid $\sigma(x) = \tfrac12(1+\tanh(x/w))$,
whose slope at the switch is $1/(2w)$ and which goes from 12\% to 88\% over $\pm w$. The state $s$ becomes a continuous
variable with its own ODE:
\begin{equation}
  \tau\dot s = \sigma(a_L - a_{\text{mean}})(1-s) - \sigma(a_{\text{mean}} - a_U)\,s + \text{latch}(s),\qquad
  \mathbf a_{\text{thrust}} = T\,\theta(s)\,\hat{\mathbf v},\quad \theta(s) = \sigma\!\Big(\frac{s-0.9}{0.02}\Big),
\end{equation}
with $a_L = 6921.9$\,km, $a_U = 6922.4$\,km, $\tau = 60$\,s and $T = 2\times10^{-4}$\,m/s$^2$. Between the edges both
switches are off and $s$ keeps its value: a \textbf{hysteresis}, so the same SMA can belong to a coast or a burn.
\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{Coefficients of the sigmoidal thrust law.}\label{tab:sigmoid}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{llX}
\toprule
Coefficient & Value & Role\\\midrule
switch width $w$ & 5\,m of SMA & how sharply on$(a)=\sigma(a_L-a)$ and off$(a)=\sigma(a-a_U)$ switch (slope $1/2w$)\\
switch time $\tau$ & 60\,s & time constant of $s$: how fast the thruster state follows the switches\\
latch gain $\lambda$ & 1 & latch$(s)=\lambda s(1-s)(2s-1)$: zero at $s = 0, \tfrac12, 1$; pushes $s$ away from $\tfrac12$, so
$s = 0$ and $s = 1$ are the two stable states (memory)\\
throttle centre $s_0$ & 0.9 & the engine opens only once the latch has committed to ``on'' ($s>\tfrac12$)\\
throttle width $w_\theta$ & 0.02 & $\theta(s)=\sigma((s-s_0)/w_\theta)$ opens over $s\approx0.88$--$0.92$: a $\sim$30-s thrust ramp\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}
If the throttle opened at $s = \tfrac12$, the first thrust would lift the SMA back above $a_L$ before $s$ had committed and
the burn would stall half-way; the offset $s_0 = 0.9$ prevents this.

\paragraph{State-space form.} Reduced to the two slow states $\mathbf x = (a, s)$ (the orbit phase does not enter the
law), the controlled system is
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathbf x} = \begin{pmatrix}\dot a\\ \dot s\end{pmatrix}
  = \begin{pmatrix} -c_d\,D(a,v) + T\,\theta(s)\,B(a,v)\\[2pt]
    \tau^{-1}\big[\sigma(a_L-a)(1-s) - \sigma(a-a_U)\,s + \lambda s(1-s)(2s-1)\big]\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with $D = (2a^2/\mu)|\mathbf v|^3$ and $B = (2a^2/\mu)|\mathbf v|$ the drag and thrust shapes.
Inside the band the second row is just the latch, with stable points $s=0$ and $s=1$: two branches, coasting
($\dot a = -5.3$\,m/h) and burning ($\dot a = +1311$\,m/h). The edges are where a branch loses stability and the state jumps
to the other one (Figure~\ref{fig:field}). A classifier only has to tell which branch a sample is on --- which is exactly
what the GMM does with the acceleration feature, and what the hysteresis edges do with the SMA (Figure~\ref{fig:rateloop}).
\fig{step21_deep_dive/step21_hysteresis_field.png}{The controller as a two-state ODE: streamlines of the vector field
in the $(a, s)$ plane (directions only) and one true cycle. Coasting runs left along $s=0$, at $a_L$ the state is
pushed up to $s=1$, burning runs right along $s=1$, and at $a_U$ it drops back: a clockwise hysteresis loop.}{fig:field}
\fig{step21_deep_dive/step21_hysteresis_rate.png}{The same loop in the $(a, \dot a)$ plane, with arrows for the
direction of travel and each sample coloured by the GMM's $P(\text{burn})$: the classifier splits the loop into its two
branches.}{fig:rateloop}

\end{itemize}

\section{Method selection: bias--variance tuning (step 0)}\label{sec:tuning}
% =====================================================================================================
Every adjustable setting (stencil order, smoothing window, number of mixture components, sparsity threshold)
is chosen from data rather than by hand. Over repeated noise draws the mean squared error of an estimate splits
into \cite{hastie}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E[(\hat y - y)^2] = (\mathbb E[\hat y]-y)^2 + \mathbb E[(\hat y-\mathbb E[\hat y])^2] = \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance},
\end{equation}
and the tuned setting minimises the sum (Table~\ref{tab:tuning}). The choices are saved to a settings file
in the cache folder and read by every later step.

\section{Stage 1: estimating the natural dynamics and $c_d$ (steps 2--4)}\label{sec:cd}
% =====================================================================================================
Before a burn can be seen, the known physics has to be removed, and the one unknown that matters most for the
forecast is the drag coefficient. Five estimators were compared.

\subsection{Methods}
\paragraph{Finite-difference (FD) regression.} Differentiate the positions twice with a central stencil of order
$p$ (truncation error $O(h^p)$, noise amplification $\propto\sigma/h^2$) and solve a linear least-squares problem
in which each unknown multiplies a known shape:
\begin{equation}
  \ddot{\mathbf r} + \frac{\mu\mathbf r}{r^3} = [c_d,\ J_2,\ \mu_{\text{Moon}},\ \mu_{\text{Sun}},\ PC_RA/m]\cdot[\text{shapes}],
\end{equation}
with the columns scaled to unit norm (they differ by ten orders of magnitude).
\paragraph{Energy method.} One derivative only: the mean SMA decays at $\dot a = -(2a^2/\mu)c_d|\mathbf v|^3$, so
$c_d$ follows from the slope of a straight line through the whole SMA history, which averages all the noise.
\paragraph{Shooting.} Propagate with trial parameters and fit the whole trajectory by Levenberg--Marquardt
\cite{scipy}, started from the energy answer.
\paragraph{Extended Kalman filter (EKF).} State $(\mathbf r,\mathbf v,c_d)$, propagated with its state-transition
matrix and updated by every measurement \cite{kalman}.
\paragraph{Force spectroscopy.} Identify each force by the frequency band where it holds $\ge$90\% of its power
and read its strength from the band area (Parseval) \cite{numpy}.

\section{Stage 2: detecting burns with a Gaussian mixture model (steps 6--7)}\label{sec:gmm}
% =====================================================================================================
\subsection{The feature: the unmodelled acceleration}
From the measured states, the velocity derivative minus the known physics leaves only what is not modelled:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf a_u = \dot{\mathbf v}_{\text{measured}} - \Big(-\frac{\mu\mathbf r}{r^3} + \mathbf a_{J_2}\Big) \approx \mathbf a_{\text{drag}} + \mathbf a_{\text{thrust}} + \text{noise}.
\end{equation}
The derivative is a Savitzky--Golay filter \cite{savgol} (least-squares polynomial of order 5 through 61 samples).
Two features are used: $x_1 = \log|\mathbf a_u|$ (how much unmodelled acceleration) and
$x_2 = \mathbf a_u\cdot\hat{\mathbf v}/10^{-4}$ (thrust pushes forwards; noise has no direction).

\subsection{The subtraction, and how much of the residual is numerical}

Removing 8.3\,m/s$^2$ to see $8\times10^{-7}$\,m/s$^2$ is a cancellation of seven decades, so the residual could be
contaminated by floating-point round-off or by the integrator. Each numerical source was estimated on noise-free data
(Figures~\ref{fig:cascade} and~\ref{fig:numerr}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{machine epsilon}: $\epsilon\,|\mathbf g| = 2.2\times10^{-16}\times8.3 = 1.8\times10^{-15}$\,m/s$^2$ for the
        subtraction, and $\epsilon|\mathbf v|\,\|\mathbf c\|_2 = 9\times10^{-15}$ for the Savitzky--Golay derivative;
  \item \textbf{solver tolerance} ($10^{-11}$) passed through the derivative filter: $4\times10^{-10}$;
  \item \textbf{SG truncation}, measured by applying the same filter to an exact Kepler orbit: $8.3\times10^{-9}$;
  \item \textbf{total} numerical error of the clean feature: $1.0\times10^{-8}$\,m/s$^2$.
\end{itemize}
The numerical floor is therefore set by the derivative filter's truncation, is \textbf{80 times below the drag} and 460
times below the derivative noise at the reference noise, and is $10^{7}$ times above machine epsilon. Its smooth
twice-per-orbit pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:numerr}) is the filter's truncation of the orbit curvature, not solver noise.
The ``choppy'' appearance of the GMM histogram (Figure~\ref{fig:gmmhist}) therefore has a statistical, not numerical,
cause: in the tails each bin holds only a handful of the 20\,000 samples, so its height has a Poisson error of
$\pm\sqrt n/n$, which is 30--100\% in the tails (Figure~\ref{fig:tail} shows these error bars).

\subsection{The derivative: Savitzky--Golay filters}\label{sec:sg}
A Savitzky--Golay (SG) filter \cite{savgol,schafer} fits a polynomial of order $p$ by least squares to the $2m+1$ samples
around each point and differentiates the polynomial at the centre. With $A_{ji} = (jh)^i$ for $j = -m..m$ and
$i = 0..p$, the fitted coefficients are $(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf y$, and the derivative at the centre is their
$i=1$ entry, so the filter is a fixed convolution
\begin{equation}
  \hat y'_k = \sum_{j=-m}^{m} c_j\,y_{k+j},\qquad \mathbf c = \big[(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\big]_{1,:},
  \qquad H(f) = \sum_j c_j\,e^{2\pi i f jh}.
\end{equation}
Three properties make it the right tool here:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No bias for smooth signals}: it is exact for polynomials up to order $p$, so it follows the
        orbit's curvature (Figure~\ref{fig:sgresp}: $|H|$ equals the ideal $2\pi f$ up to the cut-off);
  \item \textbf{Low-pass}: above the cut-off ($f_c = 0.006$\,Hz, a 2.8-min period, for the tuned 61 samples / order 5)
        it rejects the noise that finite differences amplify ($|H|$ keeps rising to the Nyquist frequency);
  \item \textbf{Known noise gain}: white noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ becomes $\sigma\|\mathbf c\|_2$; for order 5
        it falls from 0.076\,s$^{-1}$ (11 samples) to 0.0053\,s$^{-1}$ (61 samples), roughly as window$^{-3/2}$
        (Figure~\ref{fig:sggain}).
\end{enumerate}
The price is the cut-off itself: features faster than $\sim$2.8\,min, like the 30-s thrust ramps, are smeared to $\sim$60\,s
(which is why the learned rise time is 60\,s, Section~\ref{sec:thrust}). Window and order trade this bias against the
noise gain, and step 0 chose them by exactly that bias--variance balance.
\fig{step21_deep_dive/step21_sg_response.png}{Frequency response of derivative filters on 6-s data: central differences
of order 2, 4, 8 follow the ideal differentiator all the way to the Nyquist frequency (amplifying noise), SG filters
roll off above a cut-off that falls as the window grows. The orbit and the $J_2$ line are far below the cut-off.}{fig:sgresp}
\fig{step21_deep_dive/step21_sg_noise_gain.png}{White-noise gain of the SG derivative against window length and polynomial
order.}{fig:sggain}

\subsection{Why the change of basis to $(x_1, x_2)$}
The raw unmodelled acceleration is a 3-vector, best seen in the satellite's radial / along-track / cross-track frame.
In that linear frame the coast samples ($\sim10^{-6}$) collapse onto the origin next to the burns ($2\times10^{-4}$), and a
Gaussian fitted to both clusters would be dominated by the burns (Figure~\ref{fig:basisraw}). The features are a
nonlinear change of basis with a physical reason for each coordinate:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $x_1 = \ln|\mathbf a_u|$ is the \textbf{log-magnitude}: it compresses four decades into a few units, so the coast
        cloud and the burn cluster get comparable widths and a mixture can describe both; it is frame-independent.
  \item $x_2 = \mathbf a_u\cdot\hat{\mathbf v}/10^{-4}$ is the \textbf{projection on the velocity}: the thrust acts along
        $\hat{\mathbf v}$, so a burn has $x_2 \approx |\mathbf a_u|/10^{-4} \approx 2$, while noise points anywhere, so its
        $x_2$ is centred near zero (the small negative offset, $-0.009\times10^{-4}$\,m/s$^2$, is the drag).
\end{itemize}
Because $|x_2| \le e^{x_1}/10^{-4}$ (a component cannot exceed the magnitude), every sample lies inside a wedge;
burns sit on its upper edge (all of their acceleration is along-track) and the coast fills its narrow end
(Figure~\ref{fig:basisfeat}). For isotropic noise, the along-track share $x_2/(e^{x_1}/10^{-4})$ is the cosine of a random
direction, which is \emph{uniform} on $[-1,1]$ (Archimedes' hat-box theorem); the coast samples confirm it
(Figure~\ref{fig:hatbox}).

\subsection{The mixture and how it is fitted}
A GMM models the feature density as a weighted sum of $K$ Gaussians \cite{dempster,sklearn},
\begin{equation}
  p(\mathbf x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x;\bm\mu_k,\Sigma_k),
\end{equation}
fitted by expectation--maximisation on 20\,000 random TRAIN samples (three restarts). The E-step computes each
sample's \emph{responsibility} $\gamma_{ik} = \pi_k\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i;\bm\mu_k,\Sigma_k)/\sum_j \pi_j\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i;\bm\mu_j,\Sigma_j)$;
the M-step re-estimates $\pi_k = \tfrac1n\sum_i\gamma_{ik}$, $\bm\mu_k$ and $\Sigma_k$ as responsibility-weighted means
and covariances. The mixture is unsupervised: it never sees the true labels. A component is called
\textbf{burn} if its mean $x_1$ lies more than three standard deviations of the heaviest (coast) component above the
coast mean --- thrust can only add acceleration. A sample is a burn if its most likely component is a burn
component, and $P(\text{burn}\mid\mathbf x) = \sum_{k\in\text{burn}}\gamma_{k}$.

\subsection{Why the coast cloud is fat-tailed, and better-shaped mixtures}\label{sec:tails}
Figures~\ref{fig:gmmhist} and~\ref{fig:gmmscatter} show that the GMM models the acceleration only approximately: it
needs three Gaussians for one coast cloud, which is skewed, with a long tail towards small accelerations and a sharp
edge on the large side. The reason is the logarithm of a vector length. If the coast residual is noise $\mathbf n$ with
independent $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ components, $|\mathbf n|/\sigma$ has a chi distribution with $k = 3$ degrees of
freedom and $x_1 = \ln|\mathbf n|$ has the \emph{log-chi} density
\begin{equation}
  f(x_1) = \frac{2}{2^{k/2}\,\Gamma(k/2)}\exp\!\Big(k\,(x_1-\ln\sigma) - \tfrac12 e^{2(x_1-\ln\sigma)}\Big).
\end{equation}
Its left tail decays only as $e^{k x_1}$ --- a straight line on a log-density plot, i.e.\ an exponential, \textbf{fat} tail:
a 3-vector is seldom near zero, but when it is its logarithm can be very negative. Its right tail decays as
$\exp(-e^{2x_1})$, \textbf{thinner} than any Gaussian. On the 19\,923 true coast samples (Figure~\ref{fig:tail}):
skewness $-0.89$, excess kurtosis $1.71$, measured left-tail slope 2.54 (theory 3), and a free fit gives $k = 2.75$ and
$\sigma = 2.9\times10^{-6}$\,m/s$^2$ (the drag offset and a slightly anisotropic derivative noise make $k$ a little less than 3).
The log-chi beats a single Gaussian by 500 in log-likelihood with the same number of parameters.
\fig{step21_deep_dive/step21_coast_tail.png}{Coast feature $x_1$ with Poisson error bars: a single Gaussian misses both
tails; the log-chi law follows the exponential left tail and the sharp right edge.}{fig:tail}

\paragraph{A fat-tailed coast with a thin-tailed burn.} Using this physics, the coast component was replaced by
\begin{equation}
  x_1 \sim \text{log-chi}(k,\sigma),\qquad x_2\mid x_1 \sim \mathcal N\!\big(\mu_2,\ c\,e^{2x_1}/10^{-8}\big)\quad(c = \tfrac13 \text{ for isotropic noise}),
\end{equation}
and the burn kept as one Gaussian (a nearly constant forward thrust: a narrow, thin-tailed cluster). The mixture is fitted by
EM, the coast's four parameters by weighted maximum likelihood in each M-step. A Student-$t$ coast (fat tails on both
sides) with a Gaussian burn was fitted for comparison. The fitted physics mixture gives $k = 2.87$, $c = e^{-1.097} =
0.334$ --- the hat-box value $\tfrac13$ to 0.3\% --- $\mu_2 = -0.003$ (drag) and a burn weight of 0.0039
(Table~\ref{tab:mixtures}, Figure~\ref{fig:mixfits}).

\paragraph{How the number of Gaussians is chosen: BIC and AIC.} For a model with $p$ free parameters and maximised
likelihood $\hat L$ on $n$ samples,
\begin{equation}
  \text{BIC} = -2\ln\hat L + p\ln n,\qquad \text{AIC} = -2\ln\hat L + 2p,
\end{equation}
and a $K$-component GMM in $d = 2$ dimensions has $p = K\big(1 + d + \tfrac{d(d+1)}{2}\big) - 1 = 6K - 1$ parameters
(weights, means and covariances).
Each extra Gaussian costs 6 parameters, so it is worth adding only if it raises $\ln\hat L$ by more than
$3\ln n \approx 30$ (BIC, $n = 20\,000$) or 6 (AIC). Here both criteria keep falling up to $K = 7$
(Figure~\ref{fig:icfull}): each new Gaussian buys a better description of the skewed coast cloud, not a new physical
cluster \cite{schwarz}. That is why $K$ was not chosen by BIC but by what the detector needs --- the Brier score of
$P(\text{burn})$ and the event F1 (step 0) --- which settle at $K = 4$. The physics-shaped mixture reaches a BIC better
than $K = 4$ with 10 parameters instead of 23, and a held-out likelihood between $K = 4$ and $K = 5$.

\subsection{From samples to events}
A burn is about 230 samples. Flags are grouped into \textbf{events}: gaps of at most two samples are filled and
runs shorter than one minute dropped. Scoring is per event: a true burn is found if a detected event overlaps it
within $\pm60$\,s; precision = real events / detected events, recall = found burns / burns, F1 their harmonic mean.
Over the TEST days at the reference noise the GMM classifies 5.18 million samples with 21\,034 true positives,
no false negatives and 499 false-positive samples, all on the burn ramps --- they merge into the 92 events, so the
event score is perfect.

\paragraph{Why a Gaussian process.} After stacking we have about 33\,000 noisy samples (330 per burn, from 3\,min
before to 30\,min after each start) of one unknown curve
$g(\Delta t)$, the thrust against the time since the burn started. We want (i) a smooth estimate of $g$ without assuming
its shape (the operator's ramp, plateau and cut-off are unknown), (ii) an uncertainty band, and (iii) the level, the
duration and the rise time read off the curve. A Gaussian process \cite{rasmussen} gives all three: it is a prior over
smooth functions, $g \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, k)$, with
\begin{equation}
  k(\Delta t, \Delta t') = \sigma_f^2\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(\Delta t-\Delta t')^2}{2\ell^2}\Big) + \sigma_n^2\,\delta_{\Delta t\Delta t'},
\end{equation}
whose length scale $\ell$, signal variance $\sigma_f^2$ and noise variance $\sigma_n^2$ are fitted by maximising the
marginal likelihood (giving $\ell \approx 63$--$64$\,s). Because exact GP regression costs $O(n^3)$, a random 1\,500 of the
stacked samples are used for the fit (\code{GP_MAX_POINTS}). Conditioned on the data $\mathbf y$ at times $X$, the prediction at
$\Delta t_*$ is Gaussian with
\begin{equation}
  \bar g_* = \mathbf k_*^\top (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1}\mathbf y,\qquad
  \operatorname{var} g_* = k(\Delta t_*,\Delta t_*) - \mathbf k_*^\top (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1}\mathbf k_*,
\end{equation}
with $K_{ij} = k(X_i,X_j)$ and $(\mathbf k_*)_i = k(X_i,\Delta t_*)$. It was preferred to a spline (no uncertainty, knots
to choose) and to a neural network (far more data needed, no calibrated band). Its limitation is that it knows nothing
about switching: a stationary RBF kernel must use one length scale for both the flat plateau and the sharp ramps, so it
rings slightly at the corners. A \emph{parametric} sigmoid law, the same form as the simulator's thrust model
(Section~\ref{sec:thrustmodel}),
\begin{equation}
  g(\Delta t) = P\,\sigma\!\Big(\frac{\Delta t - t_{\text{on}}}{r}\Big)\,\sigma\!\Big(\frac{t_{\text{off}} - \Delta t}{f}\Big) + b,
\end{equation}
has only 6 parameters and fits the stacked burns slightly better (Figures~\ref{fig:thrustmodels}--\ref{fig:thrustres}).
The GP stays useful when the shape is not known in advance.

\subsection{SINDy and BINDy}
SINDy \cite{brunton} writes a rate as a sparse combination of candidate functions,
$\dot a = \Theta(a,s)\bm\xi$, and finds $\bm\xi$ by sequentially thresholded least squares (STLSQ): solve, zero the
small coefficients, refit, repeat. Our BINDy is the Bayesian counterpart by automatic relevance determination
\cite{tipping}: each coefficient gets a Gaussian prior whose precision is learned, so irrelevant terms are pruned
and every kept coefficient has a posterior standard deviation (in the spirit of \cite{bindy,champneys}). The input is the
mean SMA (from the measured states), its smoothed derivative, and the GMM burn flags.

\paragraph{The physics library is singular.} With drag shape $D = (2a^2/\mu)|\mathbf v|^3$ and thrust shape
$B = (2a^2/\mu)|\mathbf v|$, $\dot a = -c_dD + T\theta B$. Over a 500\,m band $D$ and $B$ are constant to a few ppm and
correlated at 0.997, so $[D, B, 1]$ cannot be separated. Step 9 therefore uses cluster indicators,
$\Theta = [1-s,\ s,\ a-a_{\text{ref}},\ s(a-a_{\text{ref}}),\ (a-a_{\text{ref}})^2]$; STLSQ keeps only the two cluster terms,
from which $\hat c_d = 1.0005\,c_d$ and $\hat T = 0.9997\,T$.
\fig{step9_sindy_control_law/step9_library.png}{The drag shape, the thrust shape and the constant move together
over the band: a physics library is singular, a cluster-indicator library is not.}{fig:library}
\fig{step9_sindy_control_law/step9_coefficients.png}{SINDy and BINDy coefficients of the cluster-indicator library
with BINDy's $\pm2\sigma$: only the coast and burn terms survive.}{fig:coeffs}

\subsection{Four ways to split the learning problem (step 19)}

\subsection{Can a PCA frame help learn the coasting dynamics?}\label{sec:pca}
SINDy and BINDy learn in whatever coordinates they are given. A principal component analysis (PCA) of the coasting
state would give a data-driven frame: with $\mathbf x = (\mathbf r,\mathbf v)$ standardised, $\mathbf z = W^\top\mathbf x$ with
$W$ the right singular vectors, learn $\dot{\mathbf z} = \Theta(\mathbf z)\bm\xi$ there, and map the forecast back with
$\mathbf x = W\mathbf z$. On the drag-only run (2 days of 1-min data for training, 10 days of free-running forecast, reference
noise), five models were compared (Table~\ref{tab:pca}).
\begin{table}[H]\centering\small

\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
Model & terms & 1 orbit & 1 day & 10 days & SMA after 10 d\\\midrule

PCA, linear, 6 modes & 13 & 18\,km & 69\,km & 1817\,km & $-130$\,km\\
PCA, linear, 4 modes & 4 & 16\,km & 67\,km & 1817\,km & $-131$\,km\\
PCA, quadratic, 4 modes & 6 & 8.4\,km & 90\,km & 2104\,km & $-247$\,km\\
Cartesian, physics library $[\mathbf r/r^3, \mathbf a_{J_2}, -|\mathbf v|\mathbf v]$ & 3 & 0.9\,m & 7\,m & 0.65\,km & $-1.3$\,m\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PCA finds the right structure}: two modes hold 99.9\% of the variance (one oscillator pair, the orbit in
        its plane), two hold 0.04\% each (the slow $J_2$ precession of the plane) and two are empty. In that frame the
        linear dynamics are sparse (4 terms against 42 in Cartesian coordinates) and their eigenvalues,
        $\pm15.03\,i$\,TU$^{-1}$, are the orbital rate $2\pi\,\mathrm{TU}/T_{\text{orb}}$.
  \item \textbf{But a linear change of frame cannot fix a nonlinear law.} A least-squares linear model fits the same
        dynamics in any frame ($A_z = W^\top A W$); PCA only truncates and sparsifies. The coasting dynamics are nonlinear
        ($1/r^2$ gravity, $J_2$ at twice the orbital frequency, drag $\propto|\mathbf v|\mathbf v$), so every linear or
        quadratic model drifts by tens of km within a day, and the PCA models drift faster than the dense Cartesian one
        because truncation and thresholding drop small but real couplings.
  \item \textbf{The right frame is the physical one.} The three-term physics library recovers $\mu$ to $2\times10^{-8}$, $J_2$
        to $10^{-5}$ and $c_d$ to 0.1\% from two days, and stays within 0.65\,km after 10 days. For the slow dynamics that the
        forecast needs, the mean-element frame used in Sections~\ref{sec:sindy}--\ref{sec:forecast} (one scalar, the
        mean SMA, whose rate is the drag) is a nonlinear change of coordinates that does what PCA cannot.
\end{itemize}
BINDy (ARD) in the 6-mode PCA frame keeps 2--5 terms per equation, confirming the sparsity, but cannot add the missing
nonlinear terms. PCA is therefore useful as a diagnostic (it exposes the oscillator pair and the precession) rather than
as a modelling frame for the coast.

\subsection{A calibrated mean-element model}
Hundreds of scenarios need a fast model. The one the controller follows is
\begin{equation}
  \dot a = -k\,w(t)\,e^{-(a-a_{\text{ref}})/H} + T\,s,\qquad s: 0\to1 \text{ at } a\le a_L,\ 1\to0 \text{ at } a\ge a_U,
\end{equation}
with $k = 5.335$\,m/h, $T = 1311$\,m/h net, $H = 60$\,km and the space-weather factor $w(t)$, calibrated on the full run
(burn period within 0.01\%).

daily proxy (F10.7/Ap-like \cite{picone}) has 5\% noise.

\subsection{Step 4b revisited: spectral mapping of the forces}
Besides the time-domain regressions, the forces can be mapped in frequency, an idea borrowed from infrared
spectroscopy: every perturbation has its own lines (Figure~\ref{fig:specmap}). $J_2$ and the third bodies sit at 2 cycles
per orbit (and 1 and 3 for the Moon), SRP at 1 cycle per orbit, drag near zero frequency. A force is identified by a
\emph{diagnostic band} that holds $\ge$90\% of its power, and its strength is read from the band area against a reference
library. By Parseval's theorem $\sum|X_k|^2 = N\,\overline{a^2}$, so the \emph{square root} of the band power, an RMS, is what
matches the force budget; the power itself squares the amplitudes and turns $J_2$'s 99.98\% into 99.999999\%
(Figure~\ref{fig:speccomp}). The band reading recovers drag to 0.5\%, $J_2$ to 2\%, SRP to 0.9\% and the Moon to 8\%; the
Sun, whose lines lie under the Moon's, is not separable. It is 30--40 times faster than regression (it needs no force
model along the orbit), which makes it a quick survey tool for a large catalogue. The same spectra define where the $J_2$
line can be seen at all: the SNR surface over sampling step and noise (Figure~\ref{fig:snrsurf}) drops below 10\,dB once
the step exceeds the Nyquist limit $T_{\text{orb}}/4$ or the noise exceeds a few hundred metres.
\fig{step4_spectral_kalman/step4_force_spectra.png}{Spectral map of the forces (radial acceleration, normalised): each
force has its own lines; a 360-day record (solid) resolves them far more sharply than 15 days (dashed).}{fig:specmap}
\fig{step4_spectral_kalman/panels/step4_spectroscopy_spectrum_a.png}{A 120-day force spectrum with the diagnostic band of
each force shaded.}{fig:specband}
\fig{step4_spectral_kalman/step4_spectroscopy_composition.png}{Composition read from the spectrum: the square root of the
power matches the force budget, the power does not.}{fig:speccomp}
\fig{step4_spectral_kalman/spectral_snr_surface_3d.png}{$J_2$ SNR over sampling step and noise (15 days): both slow
sampling and noise bury the line; the red floor contour marks 10\,dB.}{fig:snrsurf}
\fig{step4_spectral_kalman/step4_spectroscopy_speed.png}{Speed of the spectral reading against regression for growing

\subsection{Step 12: conjunction screening}

brief's ``hundreds of km a week''. The simulated drag-only RMS (396\,km) is below this bound, as it must be: the burn

\end{checkbox}

\paragraph{Notes on the forecasting rank.} The periodic schedule is the most accurate when drag is constant but the
least robust: under space weather it is the worst, which is the realistic case, so its robustness score is 1. The GP
coast curve beats the constant-rate law on accuracy with fine data but degrades from 1-min sampling; the law is the
form that accepts a space-weather proxy, which is what makes the top-ranked forecaster.
\paragraph{Pipeline recommendation.} Savitzky--Golay (or one-step propagation above 2\,min) $\to$ GMM detection $\to$
drag on clean coast arcs (mean SMA, or an EKF on sparse data) $\to$ thrust on clean burn arcs (with a GP for the shape)
$\to$ hysteresis edges $\to$ a law forecast driven by a space-weather proxy, with conformal intervals.

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Steps 1--9 and 19--20 use full state vectors with 0.1\,m / 0.5\,mm/s noise, far better than real tracking; step 11
        shows how fast detection degrades with realistic passes.
  \item Constant drag in the 400-day run flatters the periodic schedule; step 10 corrects this with modelled, not
        measured, space weather.
  \item Steps 10--16 use the calibrated mean-element model, not full integration.
  \item No real ephemerides were analysed (an account with a data provider is needed); angles-only orbit determination
        is not implemented.
  \item The method ranking is a judgement from the numbers above, not an independent benchmark.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}
Run the chain on real Starlink ephemerides (CCSDS OEM \cite{ccsds}); replace the constant-rate law by the event-driven
law of step 10 inside the end-to-end chain; learn the density model jointly with the control law; and couple the
manoeuvre forecasts to a real conjunction screening and sensor-tasking loop.

\section{Glossary}
\begin{description}[style=nextline]
  \item[SMA] semi-major axis; \emph{osculating} from the two-body energy, \emph{mean} including the $J_2$ potential.
  \item[Deadband, hysteresis] the allowed SMA band; the controller's memory of whether it is burning.
  \item[GMM, EM] Gaussian mixture model, expectation--maximisation; responsibility = posterior of a component.
  \item[Event F1] harmonic mean of event precision and recall.
  \item[SINDy / STLSQ] sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics / sequentially thresholded least squares.
  \item[BINDy / ARD] the Bayesian variant / automatic relevance determination.
  \item[GP] Gaussian process; EKF = extended Kalman filter.
  \item[$P_c$] collision probability; conformal prediction = distribution-free calibrated intervals.
\end{description}
